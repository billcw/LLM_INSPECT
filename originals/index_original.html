How an LLM predicts the next token

SmolLM2-135M — 134,515,008 parameters, 30 layers, 576-dimensional vectors, 49,152-token vocabulary. Running locally on CPU.
Start here: what this model actually is

This model does exactly one thing: given some text, it produces a probability for every one of the 49,152 tokens in its vocabulary, describing how likely each is to come next.

That's the whole model. It doesn't plan a sentence. It doesn't look anything up. It computes one distribution over possible next tokens and stops. Everything else below — whole sentences, randomness, apparent creativity — is built on top of that one operation by repeating it.

Baseline to keep in mind: pure guessing would give every token 1 / 49,152 = 0.00203%. Watch how far the real numbers are from that.

1. The prompt

Tokens update as you type (tokenizer only — the model is not run). Press Enter or click Predict to run the model.
Tokens, not words — why the text gets chopped up

The model can't read letters or words. Your text is first split into tokens — chunks from a fixed vocabulary of 49,152 entries, built by an algorithm called BPE (byte-pair encoding).

Common words are usually one token; rare words get split. Most tokens include a leading space — " capital" is a different token from "capital".

So when you see " Os" or " Sh" in the predictions, the model isn't confused — it's starting to spell Osaka or Shizuoka and will finish on the next pass.

Logits, softmax, and what temperature actually changes

The model's raw output is 49,152 numbers called logits — unbounded scores, one per token. They aren't probabilities; they can be negative and don't sum to anything in particular.

Softmax turns them into probabilities: exponentiate each, divide by the total. Everything becomes positive and the set sums to 1.

Temperature divides the logits first: p = softmax(logits / T) Small T spreads them apart so softmax concentrates; large T squashes them together so it flattens. At T = 0 it collapses onto the single highest logit.

The ranking never changes. Dividing every logit by the same positive number preserves their order, so the ten tokens listed below are identical at every temperature above zero — and in the same sequence. Only the probabilities move.

Any rank from 1 to 49,152. The browser already holds the full sorted list, so this costs no forward pass. Try 5,000 and 40,000 to see how far the ranking extends.

How one token actually gets picked

The number line, and the dart

Lay all 49,152 tokens end to end on a line from 0 to 1, each occupying a segment as wide as its probability. Then throw one dart.

1. Softmax the logits → 49,152 probabilities summing to 1
2. Cumulative sum them → the segment boundaries below
3. Draw one uniform random number r in [0, 1)
4. Take the first token whose boundary exceeds r

That's the entire selection mechanism. If you imagined raffle tickets — one duplicate per unit of probability, drawn blindly from a hat — you had it essentially right. The number line is the same idea without needing to count tickets: a token at probability 0.000001 would need a million tickets, but it just gets a segment 0.000001 wide.

The dart knows nothing. The random number generator has never seen the prompt, the logits, or the tokens. It produces a number between 0 and 1 and nothing else. All the intelligence is in the segment widths — and the model computed those.

Only the top 8 segments are drawn individually; the rest are lumped into the grey block. But the arithmetic uses all 49,152 — a dart can and will land inside the grey and resolve to a specific token.

What this widget does and doesn't share with real generation

Same algorithm, different random source. This widget uses the browser's Math.random(). Actual generation in section 3 uses torch.multinomial on the server, which is why the Seed control there works and this widget has no seed.

The segments shown are exact — computed from the full 49,152-token distribution, not just the visible ones.

3. Generation — repeating the one operation

What is a "forward pass"?

A forward pass is one complete trip through the model: tokens go in at one end, 49,152 logits come out the other. "Forward" means data flows in one direction only — input to output, no loops, no going back to revise. (The backward pass only happens during training, when errors flow backward to adjust weights. That never happens here. The weights are frozen.)

Inside one forward pass, in order:

1. Each token is looked up in the embedding table, becoming a vector of 576 numbers.
2. Those vectors travel through 30 layers side by side, one vector per token position.
3. The last position's final vector is converted into 49,152 scores.

Two things follow that surprise most people. First, nothing is stored between passes — the model has no memory of previous runs. Every pass starts from scratch, and the only reason it "remembers" earlier text is that the text is fed in again as input. Second, the amount of computation is fixed. An easy prompt and a hard prompt cost exactly the same. The model can't think longer about a harder question.

Step 2 in detail: the residual stream (vectors get added to, not replaced)

A common misunderstanding: people assume the embedding assigns the vector for a token, and that's that. Or that each layer replaces it with a new one. Neither is right.

Here is what every one of the 30 layers does, in order:

h = h + attention(norm(h))
h = h + mlp(norm(h))

Look at the h + on both lines. Each sublayer computes a correction and adds it to what is already there. It does not overwrite. 30 layers × 2 sublayers = 60 additions, stacked on top of the original embedding.

This is why it's called the residual stream — the stream flows straight through from embedding to output, and layers write into it as it passes.

h_final = embedding + (60 corrections summed)

So the embedding vector is still in there at layer 30. Mathematically present, never deleted. The layers didn't replace the token's meaning; they annotated it with context.

You can verify this in section 5: on the prompts tried while building this page, the |h| column grew from roughly 2–3 at the embedding stage to several hundred by the last layer before the final norm. Run section 5 and this sentence is replaced by the values measured on your prompt.

Step 3 in detail: unembedding — how 576 numbers become 49,152 scores

At the end of a forward pass you're holding 576 numbers. You need 49,152 scores. The bridge is a matrix of shape 49,152 × 576 called the unembedding matrix.

Each row of that matrix is a 576-number vector representing one vocabulary token. The multiplication is 49,152 separate dot products:

logit for " Tokyo" = dot( h_final , row_for_Tokyo )

A dot product measures alignment. The final step asks, 49,152 times: how much does the vector I've built point in the same direction as this token's vector? The best-aligned token wins.

Specific to this model: SmolLM2 has tie_word_embeddings = True, meaning the unembedding matrix is the embedding matrix — the same table used in both directions.

Read one row → token becomes a vector (start of the pass).
Dot against all rows → vector becomes token scores (end of the pass).

So the whole forward pass is: look a token up in the table, spend 30 layers adding corrections to it, then ask the same table which token the result most resembles. Section 5 shows this directly — the embeddings row reads out your last input token at nearly 100%, because that vector is that token's row.

What happens when you click Generate

The model can't write a sentence. It only predicts one token. So generation is a loop:

1. Tokenize the prompt.
2. Run one forward pass. Get 49,152 logits.
3. Pick one token (the number line above).
4. Append that token to the input.
5. Go back to step 2 with the now-longer input.

That's autoregression — the model's own output becomes part of its next input. Generating 12 tokens means 12 complete forward passes through all 30 layers. Nothing is planned ahead.

This explains a lot of failure modes. Once a wrong token is appended, it's in the context permanently. The model can't go back and fix it.

Runs and Seed — why "random" is reproducible

Runs repeats generation with identical settings. At T = 0 every run is identical. Above 0 they diverge — same distribution, different darts.

Seed fixes the pseudo-random generator's starting state. Set a seed and sample at T = 1: perfectly reproducible. Even the randomness is a deterministic algorithm producing a fixed sequence from a starting value.

Suggested sequence: T = 0, then T = 1.0, then T = 1.0 with seed 42 twice.

5. Inside one forward pass — two lenses on 30 layers

The logit lens — reading the answer out early

Normally the model converts to vocabulary scores once, at the very end. The logit lens asks: what if we did it early?

readout(h_L) = lm_head(norm(h_L))

Take the residual stream after layer 6, apply the same final normalization and the same unembedding matrix from section 3, and see what token it points at. Do that at all 31 stages and you get a trace of the prediction assembling.

Its weakness: it pretends layers L+1 through 30 don't exist.

The Jacobian lens — accounting for the layers still to come

The J-lens fixes that blind spot. Instead of pretending the remaining layers don't exist, it approximates them with a single averaged linear map:

readout(h_L) = lm_head(norm(J_L · h_L))

J_L is a 576 × 576 matrix — the average of ∂h_final / ∂h_L over a corpus. In words: if I nudge this number in the stream at layer L, how does the final vector move, on average? One matrix multiply stands in for everything layers L+1 through 30 would have done.

The cost: the unembedding matrix already exists in the weights, so the logit lens is free. The Jacobians had to be computed with automatic differentiation over a corpus and cached — about 23 minutes for 12 prompts on this machine.

Important: both lenses are approximations, and how to tell if they're lying

Logit lens: the unembedding matrix was trained to read the final layer. Applying it to layer 6 assumes the residual stream keeps a consistent reading frame throughout. Early layers producing junk may mean "the answer isn't formed yet" — or "the lens can't read this layer." Those look identical here.

Jacobian lens: a Jacobian is a linear approximation of a very non-linear computation, averaged over a corpus that may not resemble your prompt. Twelve prompts is a small corpus. And a linear map fitted to predict the final vector will tend to look prescient by design.

The one hard check: at stage 30 the Jacobian is the identity matrix, so the J-lens must reproduce the model's real output exactly. The diagnostic at the bottom reports that error. If it isn't near zero, the entire J-lens column is unreliable.

6. Attention — which tokens looked at which

How to read this grid

This is the one view in the whole tool with genuinely meaningful axes. Both are token positions from your prompt.

Rows are the token doing the looking. Columns are the token being looked at. Cell (row i, column j) is how much of position i's attention went to position j. Brighter means more.

Every row sums to 1. Each position distributes a fixed budget of attention across the positions available to it.

The grey upper triangle is the causal mask. Position 2 cannot look at position 5, because position 5 hasn't happened yet. That's not a display choice — those values are mathematically zero. It's the same constraint that lets generation cache earlier positions instead of recomputing them.

The bottom row is usually the interesting one: it's the position that produces the next-token prediction.

Why 9 heads, and what "grouped-query attention" means here

A head is a slice, not a copy. 576 = 9 × 64: each of the 9 heads works in its own 64-dimensional subspace. The layer isn't doing the full computation nine times — it's doing nine narrow computations at once.

Why nine? One head produces exactly one set of attention weights per position, so it can look back at the context in exactly one way. Nine heads let the layer read the context nine ways simultaneously. The cost is that each read gets 64 dimensions instead of 576.

Each head's output goes through its own slice of the output matrix and is added to the residual stream. So a layer's attention step is really 9 independent writes summed together — the same additive structure as the residual stream itself, one level down.

Grouped-query attention: 9 query heads, 3 key/value heads, group size 3. The grids below are per query head, so there are 9 of them. Within a group, the three heads read from the same keys and values but write through different output slices — so GQA constrains what a group of heads can read, not what they can write. At the prompt lengths in this demo, the restriction on achievable attention patterns is essentially nil.

The point of it is the KV cache during generation: 3 × 64 × 2 × 4 bytes × 30 layers = 45 KiB per token here, versus 135 KiB if every head had its own keys and values. That matters enormously for a large model serving long contexts, and matters not at all for a six-token prompt on a laptop. You're looking at a design decision made for a situation you aren't in.

Do heads specialize? In the models researchers have studied closely, yes — previous-token heads, induction heads and positional heads are all documented. Whether any specific head in this model has a clean job is unknown; nobody has published a map of these 270 heads.

"Mean of all heads" averages the 9 grids. Useful for a quick overview, but it smooths away exactly the specialization that makes individual heads interesting.

Heads at the same layer can behave completely differently — one may put nearly all its attention on position 0 while another puts less there than uniform attention would. Step through them one at a time, or choose "max of all 9", which takes the largest weight any head gives each pair. That is the opposite of the mean's failure mode: a single specialized head shows up instead of being averaged away.

Two things you'll see that are easy to misread

The first column is often very bright, in many heads at once. Some of that brightness is forced by arithmetic. Row i has only i+1 unmasked cells, so even perfectly uniform attention would put 1/(i+1) on column 0 — 100% for row 0, 50% for row 1, down to about 17% for row 5 on a six-token prompt. Compare what you see against that baseline, not against zero.

The excess is the attention sink: a documented pattern where the first position absorbs large amounts of attention across many heads regardless of what that token actually is. The mechanism is usually attributed to softmax — every row must sum to 1, so a head with nothing in particular to attend to still has to put its budget somewhere, and position 0 is the only position every row can see. Why models learn to do this is a separate question. The best-developed answer is that the sink is a defence against over-mixing: every attention layer blends token representations together, and across enough layers, with a long enough context, that blending can run far enough to leave neighbouring positions nearly indistinguishable. Parking attention on position 0 lets a head do almost nothing on that layer, which slows the blending down and keeps the representations apart.

That account is argued in Barbero et al., Why do LLMs attend to the first token? (COLM 2025, arXiv 2504.02732). The reason to take it seriously is that they tested it by removing the sink rather than by looking at grids: deleting the beginning-of-sequence token from Gemma 7B at inference time lowered its score on every benchmark they report, and on the 4,096-token Ruler long-context task it fell from 82.57 to 0.00. They also find sinks get stronger in larger models and in models trained on longer contexts, which is what their argument predicts. Two limits before you carry any of that to the grid above. Their measurements are on models far larger than the one running here, so the argument may transfer but the numbers do not. And their sink usually sits on a dedicated beginning-of-sequence token — whether the tokenizer here prepends one is worth checking on the grid's own header tooltip. The same paper reports that a model pre-trained without such a token forms the sink on the first real word instead, so either case is covered by the account.

It is not decoration. In the experiment that named the phenomenon (Xiao et al., StreamingLLM, ICLR 2024), a sliding-window cache holding 1,024 recent tokens but no initial ones sent Llama-2-13B's perplexity to 5158.07 — total collapse. Keeping just four initial tokens alongside 1,020 recent ones restored it to 5.40. Replacing those four with plain line-break tokens restored it almost as well, to 5.60, which tells you the sink is about position, not about what those particular words mean.

So a bright first column is telling you about the head's plumbing, not about that word's meaning — but the plumbing is load-bearing, and nobody designed it. The model learned it during training, and the same paper found it in all ten models it tested — across the Llama-2, Falcon, MPT and Pythia families — and in BERT as well.

The diagonal is often bright too — positions attending to themselves. Also usually structural rather than semantic.

The interesting signal is off-diagonal, non-first-column brightness: a later token reaching back to a specific earlier token.

What this grid cannot tell you — and the control built in for it

Attention weights are half the operation. A head does two things: it decides how much weight to put on each position, and then it averages those positions' value vectors using those weights and writes the result through its output slice into the residual stream. This grid shows the weights. It does not show the values, and it does not show what got written.

So a bright cell can write almost nothing if the value vector behind it is small or points somewhere unhelpful, and a dim cell can write a lot. A bright cell is a hypothesis, not a finding.

This is a published result, not a hedge. Jain & Wallace, Attention is not Explanation (NAACL 2019), report that learned attention weights are frequently uncorrelated with gradient-based measures of feature importance, and that very different attention distributions can yield equivalent predictions. Serrano & Smith, Is Attention Interpretable? (ACL 2019), zeroed attention weights one at a time and found the highest weight predicts impact only noisily — it frequently fails to matter much, and gradient-based rankings often predict effects better than the weights themselves do.

The rebuttal matters too. Wiegreffe & Pinter, Attention is not not Explanation (EMNLP 2019), argued the claim turns on what you mean by "explanation", and proposed four tests for when attention can serve as one. The first is a uniform-weights baseline: compare the real distribution against a deliberately uninformative one. That is what the × uniform setting on the Scale control below does. It was built here as a legibility fix; it turns out to be the control the rebuttal recommends.

Scope, honestly: all three papers studied text classifiers with a single attention layer, not 30-layer decoder-only transformers like this one. The mechanical argument carries over unchanged — weights are not values in any architecture. The specific measurements do not. Nobody has run these tests on this model.

Where the meaning actually is: section 5. Attention decides what gets read; the residual stream is what gets written, and the two lenses up there are reading it. This section shows the scaffolding. That one shows the thing being built.

The default view — layer 30, mean of all 9 — is the worst place to look for meaning, because averaging hides the differences between heads. Step through individual heads at a fixed layer, or pick max of all 9. Then switch Scale to × uniform to see which cells are actually above what uniform attention would give. The Japan → France swap is worth trying head by head; whether any head visibly reroutes is something to check rather than assume.

What this chart is measuring

The grid above shows one layer at a time. This chart shows all 30 at once, using the data already in your browser — no extra model runs.

For every layer it takes the bottom row of that layer's grid, the position that produces the next-token prediction, and plots three numbers as multiples of that row's uniform baseline:

sink — how much goes to position 0.
self — how much the position gives itself.
best other — the largest weight on anything that is neither. This is the only one of the three that could reflect meaning, but measured on this model it usually does not: on one prompt the early-layer peak was the word "the", and on another it was a comma. Hover the point to see which token it actually is before assuming it is content.

The dashed red line at 1.0× is uniform attention. Above it means more than an even split; below means less. The y-axis is logarithmic because the values usually span two or more orders of magnitude.

The multiples depend on prompt length. The bottom row of an n-token prompt has n unmasked cells, so its uniform baseline is 1/n and no cell can exceed n× — that is one cell holding 100%. The dashed ceiling line marks it. A 6-token prompt caps at 6× where a 26-token prompt caps at 26×, so do not compare heights between charts of different prompts. Compare shapes, or the raw percentages in the hover text, which are on the same 0–100% scale everywhere.

It follows the Head selector, so you can compare the mean of 9, the max across heads, and individual heads. The vertical marker shows which layer the grid above is displaying.

Knockout — remove a head and re-run

Why this is a different kind of evidence from the grid

Everything above this point is observational. You looked at where the attention went and formed a hypothesis. The panel two headings up explains why that can only ever be a hypothesis: the grid shows the weights and not the values, so a bright cell can write nothing into the residual stream and a dim one can write a lot.

This button does the other thing. It removes a head's write — not its weights, its actual contribution to the residual stream — and runs the forward pass again. Mechanically: the nine heads each produce a 64-number slice, the slices are concatenated into 576 numbers, and one matrix maps that back into the stream. Zeroing one head's 64 columns before that matrix removes exactly that head and nothing else, because nothing has mixed across heads yet.

It measures every head at the layer, not just the one you suspect. That is the whole point. "Removing head 9 moved 4% of the probability mass" means nothing on its own. It starts to mean something when you can see that removing head 4 moved 0.3%, and that removing all nine at once moved rather more than the nine individual effects added up.

The two columns to read against each other are column-0 share — the same number the grid prints for the bottom row, first column — and mass moved. If a bright first column told you a head was important, those two would rise and fall together. Whether they do on this model, at this layer, on your prompt, is the experiment. Nine points is not enough to fit anything to; look at whether the ordering roughly agrees, and do not compute a correlation from it.

What this is not. Removing a head removes everything that head does, sink and content alike, so this cannot tell you that the sink specifically mattered. It also cannot tell you what the head was for. A head can be load-bearing for reasons that have nothing to do with the cell you were looking at.

Do not expect a dramatic failure at this length. The long-context collapse result (Xiao et al.) is measured at tens of thousands of tokens against a sliding window; this page caps at 32. And on Barbero et al.'s account a head resting on position 0 is deliberately writing almost nothing, so removing it should cost almost nothing. Those two predictions point in opposite directions, which is exactly why it is worth running rather than asserting.
